3.1 — AI Foundations

By the end of this lesson: you can explain in plain terms how the AI tools you use actually work, and why a confident answer is not the same thing as a correct one.

1Hook

An AI tool gives you a detailed, well-structured, completely wrong answer — with a citation to a paper that doesn't exist.

How did it manage to sound so sure while being so wrong?

2Core idea

Most of the AI tools you'll use are, underneath, doing one thing extremely well: predicting what text is most likely to come next, based on patterns in an enormous amount of writing they were trained on.

That's worth sitting with, because it explains almost everything else. The tool is optimised to produce text that looks like a good answer. It is not checking whether the answer is true — it has no separate store of verified facts to check against.

Fluency and accuracy are produced by completely different processes. These tools are excellent at the first one. That's why the second one is still your job.

When a tool states something false with total confidence — an invented statistic, a fabricated source, a plausible-sounding date — that's usually called a hallucination. It isn't the tool malfunctioning. It's the tool doing exactly what it does: producing text that fits the pattern of a correct answer.

3Worked example — trust audit

// Below is AI-generated text of the kind you might get for an essay. Every underlined claim reads as equally authoritative. Click each one to see which actually are.

The Cape Floristic Region is one of the world's six floral kingdoms and is located entirely within South Africa. It is the smallest of the six floral kingdoms. Recent surveys have recorded exactly 9,983 vascular plant species within its boundaries.

The region was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004. A comprehensive review by Naidoo & Petersen (2019) in the Journal of Southern African Botany, vol. 47, established the current conservation baseline.

Habitat loss from agriculture is widely regarded as the primary driver of species decline in the region.

0 of 5 claims checked

The point of the exercise: nothing in the writing itself tells you which claims are sound. The invented statistic is more specific than the true one. The fake citation is formatted more carefully than the real fact. Confidence, specificity, and good formatting are stylistic features — they carry no information about accuracy.

Cheikh Anta Diop

1923 – 1986 · Historian, physicist, Egyptologist

Diop built his entire career on a demanding standard: don't accept a confident, widely-repeated claim just because it sounds authoritative — go back to the primary evidence and check it yourself. He challenged some of the most established assumptions in the history of Africa by doing exactly that, drawing on linguistics, archaeology, and radiocarbon dating rather than deferring to consensus.

That's precisely the habit this lesson is building. AI tools are extremely good at producing answers that sound authoritative, regardless of whether they're accurate. Diop's method was a rejection of that shortcut: fluency and confidence are not evidence. If you want to know whether something is true, you go and check.

3.2 — Application Skills

By the end of this lesson: you can use an AI tool competently and critically on a real task in your own discipline.

1Hook

Same task, two prompts.

Prompt A

explain this article

Produces a generic summary that could apply to almost anything.

Prompt B

I'm a second-year psychology student preparing for a seminar. Summarise this article's central argument in 150 words, then list the three pieces of evidence the author uses to support it.

Produces something you can actually take into the seminar.

Which one would actually help you study — and what specifically makes the difference?

2Core idea

Good AI use isn't a trick or a magic phrase. It's a short, repeatable process — and most people skip three quarters of it.

Step 01 Scope Say who you are, what you need, in what form, and how long.
Step 02 Iterate Treat the first response as a draft. Push back, narrow, redirect.
Step 03 Verify Check every fact, figure, quote, and source independently.
Step 04 Refine Rewrite in your own words. Keep what survived; cut what didn't.
The first response is never the answer. It's the beginning of a conversation you're supposed to lead.
3Worked example

Task: generate study questions from a set of lecture notes.

Scope — "I'm preparing for a first-year statistics exam. From these notes, write 8 short-answer questions that test understanding rather than recall. Mark which concept each question targets."

Iterate — the first set skews too easy. Follow up: "Questions 3, 5 and 7 only test definitions. Replace them with questions requiring me to apply the concept to a worked scenario."

Verify — check each question against the actual notes. Two reference a concept that isn't in them — the tool filled a gap. Remove both.

Refine — rewrite the surviving questions in the phrasing your lecturer actually uses, so they're useful practice.

6Practise

Write a well-scoped prompt for a real task in your own subject. Use the four-step process above — this is just the Scope step, but do it properly.

  • Does it say who's asking and for what purpose?
  • Does it specify the output format and length?
  • Would someone else get a useful result from this prompt?

3.3 — Creating AI Tools

By the end of this lesson: you can design, test and refine a reusable prompt template for a task you do regularly.

1Hook

There's probably a task you do every single week — the same shape, over and over.

Name one before you continue. That's what you'll build for.

2Core idea

Everything so far has been about using AI. This lesson is about designing how you use it — building a reusable template so you're not starting from a blank prompt every time.

A good template is just a prompt with the variable parts marked out, plus the steps that make it reliable. Once it works, you reuse it indefinitely.

The shift is small but real: from asking a tool for help to designing the process you'll use every time.
3Worked example — a reusable template
Purpose
Turn a weekly assigned reading into revision notes I'll actually use.
Template
"I'm a [year] [subject] student. From the text below, produce: (1) the central argument in 3 sentences, (2) the key evidence, (3) two questions the author leaves unanswered. Flag anything you're uncertain about rather than guessing."
Verify step
Check the central argument against the text's own conclusion. If they don't match, the summary has drifted — re-run with a narrower excerpt.
What I changed
v1 gave flat summaries. Adding "two questions the author leaves unanswered" turned it into something that helps me think rather than just recall. Adding the uncertainty flag cut invented detail noticeably.

Notice the last row. The template only became good through revision — v1 didn't work. That record of what changed and why is as much a part of the tool as the prompt itself, and it's what you'll be asked to document.

PLACEHOLDER

S. E. K. Mqhayi

1875 – 1945 · Poet, novelist, translator

Mqhayi is often called the father of Xhosa poetry. What made his work distinctive wasn't only talent — it was method. He took the traditional Xhosa praise poem, a form with deep roots and established conventions, and deliberately adapted it to new subjects and new circumstances, without losing what made the form work in the first place.

That's a good model for this lesson. Designing a reusable template isn't inventing from a blank page — it's taking a process you already understand, studying its structure the way Mqhayi studied the praise poem, and shaping a repeatable version that still does the real work. His craft was patient and iterative, refined over decades. Yours is smaller in scale, but the principle holds: build on what you understand, test it, refine it, and don't mistake a first draft for a finished tool.

5Check your design

Before you build: does your planned template meet the basic criteria?

  • It has a clear, single purpose I could state in one sentence.
  • It's genuinely repeatable — I'd use it more than once.
  • The variable parts are marked out, so I'm not rewriting it each time.
  • It includes a verification step, not just a generation step.
  • I've tested it at least twice and changed something after the first run.
6Create — your template and process log

Design and test a reusable prompt template for the task you named at the start. Document what you tried, what didn't work, and your final version.

  • Is my template built for a real task I actually do?
  • Did I record what changed between versions, not just the final result?
  • Does it include a step for checking the output, not just producing it?

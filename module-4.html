AI System Design

Modules 1–3 were about using AI well. This one is about thinking like someone who builds it — scoping a problem properly, sketching how a system would address it, and designing the loop that tells you whether it actually works. No coding required.

4.1 — Problem Scoping

By the end of this lesson: you can frame a real problem in terms an AI system could plausibly help solve — and recognise when one couldn't.

1Hook

"We should use AI to improve student wellbeing."

It sounds reasonable. It's also unbuildable as written. What's missing?

2Core idea

Most failed AI projects don't fail at the building stage. They fail at the scoping stage, because nobody insisted on a precise answer to a basic question: what exactly is the task, and how would we know if it worked?

A problem an AI system can help with is specific, has a definable input and output, and comes with an agreed way to tell success from failure.

Three tests will catch most of the bad ones:

Is it specific? "Improve wellbeing" is a goal, not a task. "Flag students who haven't accessed course materials in 14 days" is a task.

Is there a clear input and output? You should be able to say what goes in and what comes out, concretely.

Can you measure success? If nobody can say what "working" looks like in advance, you can't tell a functioning system from a broken one.

3Worked example — sort the problems

// Which of these are scoped well enough to build? Decide before you check.

"Use AI to make our department more innovative."
Not yet. No task, no input, no output, no measure. "Innovative" can't be defined precisely enough for anyone to know whether the system succeeded. This is a direction, not a problem statement.
"Given a student's submitted essay, generate three specific questions a marker is likely to ask, so the student can self-review before submitting."
Buildable. Clear input (an essay), clear output (three questions), clear purpose, and a testable measure — do markers actually ask those questions? You could evaluate this against real marking feedback.
"Predict which students will drop out, so we can intervene early."
Not yet — and this one is worth pausing on. It is technically specific, with a definable input and output. But scoping isn't only a technical question. Who sees the prediction? What happens to a student wrongly flagged? Which existing inequalities would the training data encode as "risk"? A problem can be technically well-formed and still badly scoped until those are answered.
"Given a set of lecture slides, produce a glossary of technical terms with plain-language definitions."
Buildable. Narrow, bounded, low-stakes, and easy to evaluate — a lecturer can check the glossary in minutes. Small scope like this is a feature, not a limitation: it's why the system can actually be tested.

The third one is the important one. Scoping isn't only about whether a problem is technically tractable. A system can be perfectly buildable and still be the wrong thing to build. That question — should this exist in this form? — belongs at the scoping stage, not after launch.

4.2 — Architecture Design

By the end of this lesson: you can outline, at a conceptual level, how an AI system would be structured to address a scoped problem.

1Hook

Two teams build a tool to flag likely errors in student lab reports. One puts the flags straight into the student's grade. The other shows them to the student before submission and never records them.

Same model, same accuracy. Completely different system. Where does the difference actually live?

2Core idea

"Architecture" sounds technical, but at this level it just means: what are the parts, what flows between them, and where do humans sit? You can sketch this on paper. Most of the consequential decisions are made here, not in the model itself.

The model is one component. The architecture is everything around it — and that's usually where the harm or the value comes from.

Click each layer below to see what you'd have to decide.

01 Input what goes in
  • What data does the system need, and who does it come from?
  • Was it collected for this purpose, or repurposed from somewhere else?
  • Whose data is over- or under-represented in it?
  • What's the minimum you could collect and still solve the problem?
02 Processing what the model does
  • What is the model actually being asked to produce — a classification, a ranking, a piece of text?
  • Does it need to explain its output, or is a bare answer acceptable?
  • What should it do when it's uncertain? (Guessing silently is a design choice.)
03 Human review where judgement enters
  • Does a person see the output before it has any effect — or after?
  • Can that person override it, and is overriding easy or discouraged?
  • Do they have enough context to review it meaningfully, or are they rubber-stamping?
  • This is the layer the hook scenario turns on. Same model, different placement of this box.
04 Output & action what happens as a result
  • Who sees the result, and in what form?
  • What decision does it feed into, and how reversible is that decision?
  • Is the person affected told that a system was involved?
↺  output feeds back into input — see Lesson 4.3
3Worked example

Back to the hook. Both teams built the same model. The difference is entirely in layers 03 and 04.

Team A: output flows directly into a grade. Human review sits after the consequence, if at all. A false flag costs the student marks before anyone looks at it.

Team B: output goes only to the student, before submission, with nothing recorded. Human review is the student themselves, and they can ignore it freely. A false flag costs nothing.

Same accuracy, radically different risk profile — decided by two boxes on a diagram, not by the model.

6Practise

Take the problem you scoped in 4.1. Sketch its four layers — in words or a rough diagram. Pay particular attention to where human review sits and what it can actually change.

  • Have I said specifically where a person enters the process?
  • Can that person actually change the outcome, or only observe it?
  • Did I say what the system does when it's uncertain?

4.3 — Iteration and Feedback Loops

By the end of this lesson: you can design a testing and refinement cycle for an AI-supported solution — including how you'd find out it was failing.

1Hook

A system has been running for eight months. Everyone assumes it's working. Nobody has checked, because nothing has gone visibly wrong.

Is "no complaints" evidence that it's working?

2Core idea

Silence isn't evidence. The people a system fails are often the least able to complain about it — and the least likely to be asked.

A feedback loop is the part of the design that tells you the truth about how the system is actually performing, whether or not anyone speaks up. Without one, you're not running a system; you're hoping.

Design the way you'll find out it's failing at the same time you design the thing itself. Afterwards is too late — you'll only measure what's convenient.
Phase 01 Build Smallest version that could possibly work.
Phase 02 Test Against cases you chose in advance, including hard ones.
Phase 03 Observe Watch real use — especially who stops using it.
Phase 04 Adjust Change something specific. Record what and why.
3Worked example — a test plan
System
Glossary generator: turns lecture slides into plain-language definitions of technical terms.
Success measure
A lecturer reviewing 20 generated definitions accepts at least 16 without correction.
Test cases
10 straightforward terms · 5 terms with discipline-specific meanings that differ from everyday use · 5 terms that appear nowhere in the slides (does it invent a definition, or say it can't find one?)
Failure signals
Definitions that are fluent but subtly wrong · confident definitions for terms not in the source · consistently worse output for one subject area than others.
Who I'd ask
Not just students who kept using it — specifically students who tried it twice and stopped. They know something the enthusiastic users don't.
Review point
Re-run the full test set every term, since course content and terminology change.

Note the third row. The five terms that appear nowhere in the slides are the most valuable test in the set — they check whether the system admits uncertainty or fabricates confidently. That's the Module 3 hallucination problem, now handled as a design decision rather than a user's problem to catch.

5Check your design

Before you write your plan — does it stand up?

  • I defined success before testing, not after seeing the results.
  • My test cases include ones I expect it to fail.
  • I've said what a failure would look like, specifically.
  • I've planned to hear from people who stopped using it, not only those who stayed.
  • There's a scheduled review point, not just "we'll check if problems come up."
6Create — your test and iteration plan

Write a test and iteration plan for the system you scoped in 4.1 and structured in 4.2. Use the worked example as your model.

  • Would this plan actually catch a failure, or only confirm success?
  • Have I included test cases designed to break it?
  • Is there a real review schedule, not a vague intention?

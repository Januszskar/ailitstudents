2.1 — Embodied Ethics

By the end of this lesson: you can recognise and correctly name the main ethical risks in AI systems — rather than just sensing that something feels off.

1Hook

An AI study tool consistently gives weaker, vaguer feedback to students who write in a second language or a non-dominant dialect — and better feedback to everyone else.

Something's clearly wrong here. But what exactly? Try to name it before you read on.

2Core idea

Being able to say "this feels wrong" is a start. Being able to say exactly what kind of wrong is what lets you do something about it — raise it, avoid it, or design around it.

Most AI harm isn't caused by anyone deciding to cause harm. It's built in quietly, through data, defaults, and assumptions nobody questioned.

Here are the risks worth knowing by name. Tap any one to expand it.

Bias ›

The system works better for some groups than others, usually because its training data over-represented those groups.

Hallucination ›

The tool states something false with complete confidence — invented facts, fake sources, made-up statistics.

Privacy ›

Information you enter may be stored, reused, or exposed in ways you didn't intend and can't easily undo.

Unequal access ›

The best tools cost money, data, or reliable connectivity — so they widen the gap between students who have those and students who don't.

Exclusion ›

The system simply doesn't work for some people — wrong language, wrong accent, wrong assumptions about who the user is.

Surveillance ›

Tools that monitor behaviour can shift from supporting people to watching them, often without clear consent.

Over-reliance ›

Skills you stop practising, you stop having. Convenience now can cost you capability later.

3Worked example

Back to the hook. The correct name for that problem is bias — and it's worth being precise about why. The tool wasn't designed to disadvantage second-language writers. It was trained mostly on text written by fluent speakers of a dominant dialect, so it recognises that writing as "good" and struggles to read anything else generously.

Notice what naming it precisely gets you: "this tool is biased against non-dominant dialects because of its training data" is something you can raise with a lecturer, check for in other tools, or account for in how you use it. "This tool feels unfair" isn't.

PORTRAIT
PLACEHOLDER

Frantz Fanon

1925 – 1961 · Psychiatrist and philosopher

Fanon's work is built around one uncomfortable question: how do systems quietly produce harm for some people while looking perfectly neutral to everyone else? He spent his career showing that exclusion is rarely announced outright — it's built into structures, defaults, and "the way things are done." That's exactly what makes it hard to see and easy to deny.

That's the shape of the risks in this lesson. Bias in an AI system usually isn't one bad actor making a deliberate choice — it's baked into training data and design assumptions about what "normal" looks like. Fanon's method is a direct model for how to interrogate a tool: refuse to accept that a system is neutral just because it claims to be, and ask instead who does this actually work for, and who does it quietly fail?

2.2 — Safe and Responsible Use

By the end of this lesson: you can disclose AI use correctly in your own coursework, and verify AI output before you rely on it.

1Hook

A student's essay includes a full reference list, correct formatting, and no mention anywhere that AI was used to draft two of its sections.

Nothing in it is technically false. So what's the problem?

2Core idea

Two habits make AI use responsible rather than risky. Neither is complicated — both are easy to skip.

Verify. Check anything an AI tool tells you before you rely on it, especially facts, figures, quotes, and sources. Fluent writing is not evidence of accuracy.

Disclose. Say what role the tool played. Not as an apology or a confession — as basic information your reader needs to judge the work fairly.

Disclosure isn't an admission that you did something wrong. It's what makes it possible for someone to trust what you're handing them.
3Worked example
A disclosure statement that works "I used an AI assistant to generate an initial outline for sections 2 and 3, and to suggest alternative phrasings in the discussion. All sources were located and verified by me. Two statistics initially suggested by the tool were removed after I could not verify them."
↑ Names what the tool did · names what the student did · records what was corrected
Too vague to be useful

"AI was used in the preparation of this assignment."

Used how? For what? Verified by whom? A reader learns nothing they can act on.

Specific and checkable

"AI was used to generate an initial outline for section 2. All sources verified independently; two unverifiable claims removed."

A reader knows exactly what to trust and why.

PORTRAIT
PLACEHOLDER

Tiyo Soga

1829 – 1871 · Minister, translator, writer

Soga was the first Black South African ordained as a minister in an established church, and a translator who spent years rendering major works into isiXhosa. Translation demanded painstaking honesty: he was always aware that a careless or convenient rendering could quietly distort what he was trying to carry across.

That's the same discipline this lesson asks of you. Responsible AI use means doing the equivalent of Soga's careful translation work — not accepting the first fluent-sounding version, checking it against what you actually know to be true, and being transparent about where the work came from. He stood behind the accuracy of what he produced, publicly, for readers who depended on it. A disclosure statement does the same job.

5Check your understanding

Q2. An AI tool gives you a precise-sounding statistic for your essay. What comes next?
6Practise

Draft a disclosure statement for a piece of AI-assisted work you've done recently — real or hypothetical. Use the worked example above as your model.

  • Does it say specifically what the tool did?
  • Does it say what I verified myself?
  • Would a reader know what to trust after reading it?

2.3 — Ethics by Design

By the end of this lesson: you can critique an AI tool against fairness, transparency and inclusion criteria — and propose a specific improvement.

1Hook

You've been asked to redesign an AI study tool so it works fairly for every student on your campus — not the average student, every student.

What's the first thing you'd change?

2Core idea

"Ethics by design" means building fairness in from the start rather than patching harm after it happens. It's a shift in when you ask the question, and it changes what answers are even available.

Nothing about how a tool currently works is inevitable. Someone chose it — which means someone can choose differently.

Four questions do most of the work:

  1. Who benefits?Who was this built for, and who does it serve best right now?
  2. Who's excluded?Who can't use it, can't afford it, or gets worse results from it?
  3. What's baked in?What assumptions about users, language, or "normal" are built into the data and defaults?
  4. Is it explainable?Can anyone tell why it produced a given result — or is it a black box?

PLACEHOLDER

Achille Mbembe

b. 1957 · Philosopher and historian

Mbembe's work examines how power and exclusion get built into institutions — and, just as importantly, how those institutions could be built differently. Rather than stopping at critique, his writing pushes toward the harder question: what would it actually take to design something that doesn't reproduce the same exclusions?

That's the shift this lesson asks you to make. Ethics by design isn't about patching problems after a tool has caused harm. Mbembe's habit of treating institutions — universities, states, technologies — as things that were designed by particular people with particular assumptions, and can therefore be redesigned, is the same move as the four questions above.

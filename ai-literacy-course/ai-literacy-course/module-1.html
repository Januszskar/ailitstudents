1.1 — Human Agency

By the end of this lesson: you can explain why AI is human-led, and why your own judgement has to stay central to any AI-assisted work.

1Hook

A student types a question into a chatbot and pastes the answer straight into an assignment.

Whose thinking is in that answer — theirs, or the AI's? Jot your gut answer before you carry on.

2Core idea

AI systems are built by people, trained on data chosen by people, and every output reflects human decisions made upstream — what data was used, what the model was optimised for, what guardrails were set.

AI doesn't have goals of its own — it reflects the goals of the humans who built it and the humans who use it. That means the responsibility for how it's used never leaves your hands.

Human agency is your capacity to think, decide, and take responsibility for outcomes — something no tool can do for you.

3Worked example
Agency handed away

A student uses AI to generate a full essay and submits it unchanged. The thinking, structure, and conclusions all belong to the tool.

Agency preserved

A student uses AI to stress-test their own argument, then rewrites the essay in their own words. The tool challenged their thinking — it didn't replace it.

Steve Biko

1946 – 1977 · Founder, Black Consciousness Movement

Biko was a medical student who became the founder of South Africa's Black Consciousness Movement. His central argument: before you can change your circumstances, you have to reclaim your own mind. He believed the deepest damage of oppression wasn't physical — it was psychological.

"The most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed."

That idea translates directly here. Human agency isn't just having the freedom to think for yourself — it's actively using that freedom, especially when it would be easier to let something else do your thinking for you.

1.2 — Human Accountability

By the end of this lesson: you can identify who is accountable when AI is used to support academic work, and act accordingly.

1Hook

You used an AI tool to help write a lab report. It quietly invented a statistic that turned out to be wrong — and you didn't catch it before submitting.

Who's accountable? Pick your gut answer before continuing.

2Core idea

AI tools can produce plausible-sounding errors — often called "hallucinations." The person who submits the work is accountable for its accuracy, regardless of what produced it.

Disclose AI use where it's expected. Verify anything AI produces before you rely on it. The tool doesn't carry the consequences of being wrong — you do.

John Tengo Jabavu

1859 – 1921 · Founder, Imvo Zabantsundu

Jabavu founded Imvo Zabantsundu ("Black Opinion") in 1884 — the first newspaper in South Africa owned and edited by a Black South African. He was 24. Everything he published carried his name and had to withstand public scrutiny, sometimes from the Cape Parliament itself.

That's accountability in its most literal form: standing behind what you put into the world, in public, by name. When an AI tool generates a fact or a claim and you submit it, your name is on it now — the same way Jabavu's name was on every line he printed.

M. W. B. Rubusana

1858 – 1936 · Educator, minister, founding SANNC leader

Rubusana trained as a teacher and minister before becoming one of the most influential Black South African political figures of his generation. In 1909 he became the first Black person elected to the Cape Provincial Council; in 1912 he helped found the organisation that became the ANC.

His throughline was consistent: individual advancement only mattered if it built something collective. He didn't wait for permission to help shape the public institutions of his time — he organised, ran for office, and built coalitions. Your choices about AI use and fairness are a smaller version of the same kind of act: shaping a shared system, not just making a private decision.
